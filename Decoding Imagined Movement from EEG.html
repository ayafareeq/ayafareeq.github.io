Decoding Imagined Movement from EEG ✕
Year:
2025
Category:

Type:

Dataset:

This project was a controlled, reproducible benchmark of seven decoding algorithms for motor-imagery brain–computer interfaces (BCIs), evaluated on the BCI Competition IV Dataset 2a (9 subjects, 22-channel EEG, four-class imagined movement: left hand, right hand, feet, tongue). The methodology followed the standard MI-BCI pipeline: EEG was band-pass filtered to the mu/beta sensorimotor range (8–30 Hz), epoched into a 0.5–2.5 s post-cue window, and classified using one of three algorithmic families — classical spatial filtering (CSP, FBCSP), Riemannian geometry (tangent-space projection and minimum-distance-to-mean on covariance matrices), and deep/generic time-series learning (EEGNet, ShallowConvNet, MiniRocket, InceptionTime).

All seven decoders were evaluated on identical folds through a single scikit-learn-compatible harness, under three regimes of increasing difficulty — within-subject cross-session, within-subject k-fold, and cross-subject leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) — with strict leakage control (all feature learning fit inside the training split only) and paired Wilcoxon significance testing.

Rather than propose a new algorithm, the project delivered a fair, statistically grounded comparison addressing a known reproducibility gap in the BCI literature, where methods are typically reported on inconsistent splits and preprocessing.

Highlights

  • Seven decoders, three methodological families, benchmarked on one identical, leakage-controlled evaluation harness.
  • Statistically validated findings — paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, not just raw accuracy differences, backed every comparative claim.
  • Three evaluation regimes, each answering a different practical question: how well a decoder worked for one calibrated user, whether it generalized to a new session, and whether it generalized to a new person entirely.

Results

  • Within-subject accuracy: Riemannian tangent-space classification was the strongest decoder (67.1% accuracy, κ = 0.56), significantly outperforming CSP+LDA and the deep models (p ≈ 0.008). The classical ordering — Riemannian ≥ FBCSP > CSP — matched published benchmarks on this dataset.
  • Domain-specific features mattered: generic time-series classifiers applied to raw EEG windows underperformed substantially (MiniRocket: 46.4%), indicating that EEG-specific spatial/covariance representations carried information that generic convolutional kernels did not capture as efficiently.
  • Cross-subject generalization collapsed and the ranking inverted: under leave-one-subject-out testing, accuracy dropped 18–26 percentage points across models, and the simplest method — plain CSP+LDA — generalized best (43.2%), while the richer Riemannian and filter-bank models overfit subject-specific covariance structure. The model that won on a single calibrated user was not the model suited for deployment to a new one.

Limitations

  • Deep-model results were compute-conditioned. EEGNet, ShallowConvNet, and InceptionTime were trained on CPU well below their reference epoch budgets, so their reported scores were a floor, not a ceiling, and the "classical beats deep" finding held only under this constraint.
  • Incomplete cross-subject matrix. LOSO was completed for 4 of 7 models; deep-model LOSO was GPU-scale and was not run.
  • Transfer learning was implemented and unit-tested but not benchmarked on real data.
  • Single dataset, small sample (n = 9) — limited statistical power, and six pairwise tests were reported without multiple-comparison correction.
  • No hyperparameter optimization — all models used reference/default settings, with no nested cross-validation for tuning.
  • Fixed spectral and temporal parameters (one frequency band, one time window) applied uniformly across subjects, despite per-subject tuning being a known lever on this dataset.
  • Minimal artifact handling — band-pass filtering only, no ICA or EOG regression.
  • No cross-hardware portability. A model trained on this 22-electrode montage could not decode recordings from a different EEG device; porting to new hardware would require new labelled data and retraining.
  • No domain adaptation implemented — the cross-subject gap was measured and characterized, but not addressed with the standard remedies (e.g., Riemannian alignment).
