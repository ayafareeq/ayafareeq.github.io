    Medical Image Segmentation with U-Net

    Year:
    2025
    Category:
    Computer Vision, Biomedical, Machine Learning
    Type:
    Independent Project
    Tools:
    PyTorch, segmentation-models-
    pytorch, Albumentations,
    OpenCV, scikit-learn

    A deep learning pipeline for the detection and outlining of the gastrointestinal polyps in colonoscopy images at the pixel level. Given an endoscopic image, the model produced a segmentation mask marking the exact location and shape of the polyp. This is the core capability behind computer-aided detection tools that assist clinicians during screening.

    The project implemented the U-Net architecture (Ronneberger et al., 2015) and delivered a reproducible pipeline from raw data to a trained model and quantitative evaluation, built to train and evaluate on the Kvasir-SEG benchmark of 1,000 expert-annotated images.

    Highlights

    • Full end-to-end pipeline was built — download, augmentation, training, evaluation, visualisation and single-image inference as clean, testable modules.
    • There were two model variants behind one config flag — U-Net from scratch, and a transfer-learning version with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet/EfficientNet encoder.
    • Class-imbalance-aware training was implemented — combined BCE + Dice loss, with a Tversky loss also implemented for future use.
    • Honest evaluation is built in — Dice, IoU, precision and recall as mean ± std, exact empty-mask semantics, per-image score distributions and best/median/worst panels.
    • Reproducible by design — fixed seeds, deterministic training, persisted splits and per-experiment output directories.
    • Mixed-precision training was applied, early stopping, best-checkpoint selection, unit tests, an overfit-a-batch sanity check.

    For reference, published U-Net-family results on Kvasir-SEG typically fall around 0.80-0.90 Dice and 0.70-0.82 IoU, with pretrained-encoder variants at the upper end.

    Limitations

    • 2D, single-class scope — individual frames, one lesion type, no temporal or volumetric context.
    • Single-dataset validation — cross-centre generalisation was not established.
    • No uncertainty quantification — The point-estimate probabilities were set at a fixed threshold.
    • Canonical U-Net was used rather than newer variants (Attention U-Net, U-Net++, TransUNet, nnU-Net).

    Decoding Imagined Movement from EEG

    Year:
    2025
    Category:
    Neural interfaces, biomedical,
    machine learning
    Type:
    Independent Project
    Dataset:
    BCI Competition IV-2a

    This project was a controlled, reproducible benchmark of seven decoding algorithms for motor-imagery brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), evaluated on the BCI Competition IV Dataset 2a (9 subjects, 22-channel EEG, four-class imagined movement: left hand, right hand, feet, tongue). The methodology followed the standard MI-BCI pipeline: EEG was band-pass filtered to the mu/beta sensorimotor range (8-30 Hz), epoched into a 0.5-2.5 s post-cue window, and classified using one of three algorithmic families — classical spatial filtering (CSP, FBCSP), Riemannian geometry (tangent-space projection and minimum-distance-to-mean on covariance matrices), and deep/generic time-series learning (EEGNet, ShallowConvNet, MiniRocket, InceptionTime).

    All seven decoders were evaluated on identical folds through a single scikit-learn-compatible harness, under three regimes of increasing difficulty — within-subject cross-session, within-subject k-fold, and cross-subject leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) — with strict leakage control (all feature learning fit inside the training split only) and paired Wilcoxon significance testing.

    Rather than propose a new algorithm, the project delivered a fair, statistically grounded comparison addressing a known reproducibility gap in the BCI literature, where methods are typically reported on inconsistent splits and preprocessing.

    Highlights

    • Seven decoders, three methodological families, benchmarked on one identical, leakage-controlled evaluation harness.
    • Statistically validated findings — paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, not just raw accuracy differences, backed every comparative claim.
    • Three evaluation regimes, each answering a different practical question: how well a decoder worked for one calibrated user, whether it generalized to a new session, and whether it generalized to a new person entirely.

    Results

    • Within-subject accuracy: Riemannian tangent-space classification was the strongest decoder (67.1% accuracy, κ = 0.56), significantly outperforming CSP+LDA and the deep models (p ≈ 0.008). The classical ordering — Riemannian ≥ FBCSP > CSP — matched published benchmarks on this dataset.
    • Domain-specific features mattered: generic time-series classifiers applied to raw EEG windows underperformed substantially (MiniRocket: 46.4%), indicating that EEG-specific spatial/covariance representations carried information that generic convolutional kernels did not capture as efficiently.
    • Cross-subject generalization collapsed and the ranking inverted: under leave-one-subject-out testing, accuracy dropped 18–26 percentage points across models, and the simplest method — plain CSP+LDA — generalized best (43.2%), while the richer Riemannian and filter-bank models overfit subject-specific covariance structure. The model that won on a single calibrated user was not the model suited for deployment to a new one.

    Limitations

    • Deep-model results were compute-conditioned. EEGNet, ShallowConvNet, and InceptionTime were trained on CPU well below their reference epoch budgets, so their reported scores were a floor, not a ceiling, and the "classical beats deep" finding held only under this constraint.
    • Incomplete cross-subject matrix. LOSO was completed for 4 of 7 models; deep-model LOSO was GPU-scale and was not run.
    • Transfer learning was implemented and unit-tested but not benchmarked on real data.
    • Single dataset, small sample (n = 9) — limited statistical power, and six pairwise tests were reported without multiple-comparison correction.
    • No hyperparameter optimization — all models used reference/default settings, with no nested cross-validation for tuning.
    • Fixed spectral and temporal parameters (one frequency band, one time window) applied uniformly across subjects, despite per-subject tuning being a known lever on this dataset.
    • Minimal artifact handling — band-pass filtering only, no ICA or EOG regression.
    • No cross-hardware portability. A model trained on this 22-electrode montage could not decode recordings from a different EEG device; porting to new hardware would require new labelled data and retraining.
    • No domain adaptation implemented — the cross-subject gap was measured and characterized, but not addressed with the standard remedies (e.g., Riemannian alignment).

    Real-Time Assistive Vision from a Monocular Camera

    Year:
    2026
    Category:
    Computer Vision, Assistive Technology
    Type:
    Independent Project
    Tools:
    YOLOv8, OpenCV, Python

    This project was the foundation of a real-time assistive vision aid system. Video stream from a computer's built-in camera was used to detect humans and some static indoor obstacles such as furniture and doors, distance of objects was estimated from the video stream, and a voice alert was triggered. The project was built using Python with OpenCV, Ultralytics/PyTorch, and NumPy.

    The pipeline ran four stages per frame:

    (1) Frame was acquired via OpenCV (cv2.VideoCapture)

    (2) Hybrid detection was then used, the mechanism combined a learned single-stage detector - YOLOv8-nano (Ultralytics), pre-trained on MS-COCO - for humans and common obstacles, and a model-free classical heuristic (Gaussian blur -> Canny edges -> morphological dilation -> contour extraction -> polygonal approximation, filtered by aspect ratio and area) for doors, as COCO does not cover that class.

    (3) The monocular distance was estimated using the pinhole camera model under a similar-triangles assumption, \(d = \frac{H_{\text{real}} \times f_{\text{px}}}{h_{\text{box}}}\), from a calibrated focal length and a known per-class object height.

    (4) A voice alert was triggered using the macOS say TTS (text-to-speech) engine, which ran on a background worker thread with a per-object cooldown so speech didn't block the video loop or repeat unnecessarily.

    Highlights

    • Hybrid detection - YOLOv8 was paired with a classical geometric heuristic to cover doors.
    • The depth was estimated in metres from a single camera, with no stereo rig or active depth sensor, lowering the system costs.
    • The perception and speech synthesis were run on separate threads, so they didn't interfere with each other.
    • Eyes-free and hands-free operation, the alerts were done via voice synthesis.

    Results

    • The real-time pipeline demonstrated live detection, bounding-box localization, distance readout, and synchronized spoken alerts successfully.
    • Reliable detection of people and in-vocabulary COCO obstacles (chair, couch, table, TV, refrigerator, etc.) at low frame rates on a MacBook CPU.
    • The pinhole estimator produced decent metric distances after calibration of the focal length against a reference object of known size and range.
    • Door detection was validated on tall rectangular structures via the geometric heuristic.
    • Audio delivery was reliable with a threaded queue and per-object cooldown eliminating announcement flooding and frame-loop stalls.

    Limitations

    • Qualitative results. The system was not tested against a labelled dataset or ground-truth depth, so no quantitative accuracy (mAP) or distance-error metrics were reported.
    • Because the door detection was heuristic, not learned, it was sensitive to lighting, viewpoint, and clutter, and it produced false positives on other tall rectangles (cabinets, windows, posters).
    • The distance calculations were based on assumptions; accurate results required a calibrated focal length and a fixed canonical height for each class.
    • The vocabulary was limited to the 80 MS-COCO classes.
    • With no stereo baseline or motion parallax, depth was measured purely from apparent size, which could be inaccurate.
    • There was no temporal tracking; detection was per-frame only.
    • The system performed poorly in low light, motion blur, and crowded scenes.
    • The detections were announced independently and not ranked by proximity or risk.

    Myoelectric Control from EMG

    Year:
    2025
    Category:
    Biomedical, machine learning,
    control
    Type:
    Independent Project
    Tools:
    scikit-learn, MiniRocket,
    NumPy, SciPy

    The project implemented a myoelectric pattern-recognition pipeline, decoding movement intent for upper-limb prostheses from short windows of multichannel surface EMG (sEMG). It inferred the intended hand or wrist gesture and converted the prediction into stable actuator commands.

    Formally, it learned a mapping $\phi: \mathbb{R}^{L \times C} \to {1,\dots,G}$ from an $L$-sample, $C$-channel window to one of $G$ gestures, followed by a control policy $\pi$ that temporally regularized the predictions before they drove a device. The pipeline: zero-phase Butterworth band-pass (20–450 Hz) and IIR notch conditioning -> overlapping sliding windows (200 ms / 50 ms stride) -> Hudgins time-domain features (MAV, RMS, WL, ZC, SSC) per channel -> classification, benchmarking Linear Discriminant Analysis against SVM, Random Forest, and MiniRocket (convolutional kernels on raw windows) -> confidence-based rejection and causal majority-vote smoothing.

    It was built in Python (NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, aeon, pandas, Matplotlib) and ran end-to-end on a synthetic generator with no data download.

    Highlights

    • End-to-end and reproducible: raw signal to smoothed control decisions, it is runnable offline on synthetic data and switchable to real Ninapro data via one config change.
    • Two modeling paradigms side by side: feature-engineered classical models vs. feature-free convolutional time-series classification (MiniRocket).
    • Leakage-safe evaluation: splits enforced at the repetition or subject level, never by random window, random splits let overlapping windows leak information across train and test. leave-one-subject-out gave the reliable generalization estimate.
    • Automated benchmarking: swept models x split regimes and emitted a metrics table, per-condition confusion matrices, and an accuracy chart.
    • A control layer: modeled the decision-stabilization step that separated prosthesis control from generic classification.
    • Tested: unit tests validated the feature mathematics against hand-computed values.

    Results

    On the synthetic benchmark (6 gestures, 12 channels, 4 subjects), reported as raw / policy-smoothed accuracy:

    Model Within-subject
    (repetition split)    		 Cross-subject
    (leave-one-out)
    LDA 0.94 / 0.880.44 / 0.42
    SVM 1.00 / 0.930.35 / 0.33
    Random Forest1.00 / 0.930.30 / 0.29
    MiniRocket 1.00 / 0.930.33 / 0.32

    Raw / policy-smoothed accuracy.

    Two findings: (1) Within-subject, all models saturated — which was expected, since the synthetic classes differed mainly in per-channel amplitude.

    (2) Cross-subject, the accuracy collapsed, and the simple LDA generalized at least as well as the heavier nonlinear models — model complexity did not overcome subject/domain shift.

    Limitations

    • No validated human data. Results were on a synthetic generator with near-linearly-separable classes, the approximate 0.94 within-subject score demonstrated code correctness, not clinical accuracy. Real sEMG confounds — cross-talk, non-stationarity, fatigue — were absent.
    • Offline framing. Zero-phase filtering was non-causal and unusable in real time, the code reported classification accuracy but not end-to-end controller latency, and ran no streaming loop, so the < 300 ms usability constraint was stated but unverified.
    • Accuracy did not equal usability. Evaluation covered only windowed classification metrics, omitting control-oriented measures (target-acquisition completion rate, throughput, Fitts'-law path efficiency). No proportional-control regression path was implemented.
    • No domain adaptation. The cross-subject collapse exposed the core open problem, but there was no recalibration or transfer-learning mechanism to counter electrode shift and session-to-session drift.
    • Feature/model scope. Only time-domain features were wired in (no frequency-domain, AR, or wavelet features), and no deep architectures beyond ROCKET, MiniRocket yielded only pseudo-probabilities, weakening confidence-based rejection there.
    • Amputee validation was absent. Nothing was run on Ninapro DB3/DB7; intact-subject and amputee EMG differ materially.

    Soft Robotics for Pipe Inspection

    Year:
    2024 - 2025
    Category:
    Soft robotics, sensing, control
    Type:
    MECH3895 Individual Project
    Tools:
    SolidWorks, MATLAB,
    KiCad, Arduino,
    silicone casting, 3D printing

    This project aimed at investigating the validity of using low-cost, commercially available conductive textile as reliable sensors for proprioceptive feedback in soft robotics, the investigation was done on a bio-inspired inchworm robot for pipeline inspection. Soft robots are best suited for confined, irregular tubular environments where rigid inspection tools fail, but they lack effective internal sensing because stiff sensors compromise their compliance.

    The project combined experimental sensor characterisation, numerical analysis of the silicone hyperelasticity, mechanical design and fabrication, and embedded control to build and test a three-chamber pneumatically actuated inchworm robot with an onboard endoscope.

    Highlights

    • Electromechanical characterisation of three silver-coated knitted fabrics across strain axis, sensor length and width, and signal filtering was done successfully.
    • Custom signal conditioning - constant-current sources and Sallen-Key active low-pass filters, scaled to an eight-sensor array circuits were built.
    • Mooney-Rivlin hyperelastic modelling of the silicone actuators was implemented and validated in MATLAB.
    • Discrete-time PID controller was simulated in MATLAB, deployed on an Arduino Mega alongside bang-bang logic and tested on a balloon.

    Results

    • ElectroLycra proved the most repeatable fabric, tolerant of strain beyond its rated limit due to a uniform loop structure.
    • Course-direction strain gave the most usable sensing range across all three fabrics.
    • The PID controller converged with a stable, damped response (Kp=2, Kd=0.6, Ki=1) and ran correctly on hardware.
    • Structure, actuators and three-phase locomotion logic were all designed, fabricated and coded.

    Limitations

    • The fabric resistance was very low causing near-short conditions that overloaded the conditioning circuits when embedded.
    • The use of incompatible adhesive damaged the conductive paths, and mould inaccuracies caused persistent air leaks.
    • There was no full-system demonstration due to insufficient actuator inflation. The closed-loop control was validated on a balloon instead.
    • The endoscope feed was viewed raw, with no image processing due to the project's time limitations.
